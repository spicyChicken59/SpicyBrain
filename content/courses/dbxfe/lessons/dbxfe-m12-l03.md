<!-- section:why -->

You can explain individual concepts; now make them cohere. A good customer plan should not use one metric in discovery, another in the demo, and a third in the value model.

<!-- section:understand -->

Synthesis means keeping the same decision and assumptions connected across the work. Discovery defines the problem and acceptors. Architecture proposes responsibilities and alternatives. The demo explains intended behavior. The proof of value collects evidence. The value model interprets costs and benefits with uncertainty. The handoff preserves accepted scope and open risks.

Check the joins between these artifacts. Does the demo use the agreed denominator? Does the pilot test the freshness that operations needs? Does the architecture fit the security constraints? Does the cost model include the operating responsibilities you proposed? Is the recommendation stronger than the evidence permits?

For fictional Cinderline, several designs remain defensible. A scheduled improvement may meet the immediate need; a new analytical path may support broader reuse; coexistence may reduce transition risk. The capstone rewards visible reasoning and uncertainty, not a predetermined vendor choice or an employer readiness score.

<!-- section:see -->

**Fictional consistency audit.**

| Artifact | Must stay consistent |
|---|---|
| Discovery brief | Unit defect rate and morning decision |
| Architecture | Accepted revisions, permitted path, operating owner |
| Demo | Same correction rule and denominator |
| Pilot | Same population, timing basis, and acceptance owners |
| Value model | Measured or clearly hypothetical effort/cost inputs |
| Handoff | Actual evidence and unresolved conditions |

A mismatch across rows is a concrete defect to fix before adding more detail.

<!-- section:deeper -->

Use an educational rubric to inspect discovery, technical correctness, tradeoffs, communication, measurable evidence, and uncertainty. Self-assessment is a reflection, not independent validation. If a specialist has not reviewed security or a real workload has not run, say so. A polished document should make those limitations easier to find, not harder. The capstone's full model response is one defensible submission, not the only acceptable architecture.

<!-- section:customer -->

For the closing discussion: “Our recommendation connects the agreed quality decision to a bounded design and test. Here is what we know, what we propose, which alternative remains credible, and the evidence needed before the next commitment.”

<!-- section:try -->

Before opening the capstone model, write your own six-part outline: discovery, architecture/alternative, demo/fallback, proof of value, cost/value, and follow-up. Identify the weakest evidence link and the person who can help validate it.

<!-- section:revisit -->

A strong outline keeps the one-plant quality decision central, carries the same metric and correction rule throughout, and treats source/network feasibility as conditional. It includes a rollback path, a demo that separates expected and observed output, a pilot charter with measurable criteria, a sensitivity calculation using labeled inputs, and a handoff with owners.

The weakest link may be source ordering, access, operating capacity, or benefit realization. Name it rather than adding confidence language. Use the capstone rubric to compare your reasoning and revise the weak part. Completing the exercise records your work; it does not certify role mastery.
