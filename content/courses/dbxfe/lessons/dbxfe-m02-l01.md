<!-- section:why -->

“We need real-time analytics” sounds specific, but it leaves the decision undefined. On a discovery call, your job is to understand the work behind that phrase before assigning a technology.

<!-- section:understand -->

An outcome is a change in the customer's work: a faster decision, less rework, a controlled risk, or a new capability they can use. A request is a proposed means. Start with a recent concrete episode: “When did this problem last happen? Who noticed? What could they not do? What happened next?”

Stakeholders see different costs. An operations lead may want a trustworthy defect rate by shift change. An analyst may want less spreadsheet reconciliation. A security lead may want to preserve a restricted data boundary. A sponsor may need a defensible investment decision. Discovery should expose those differences before they become late objections.

For fictional Cinderline, do not assume a delay caused lost production simply because a meeting started late. Ask for the causal chain and how the customer would measure it. Treat a stakeholder's estimate as reported, not independently observed. You can acknowledge a frustrating experience without validating an unmeasured financial claim.

<!-- section:see -->

**Fictional stakeholder map.**

| Person | Decision or concern | Evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| Plant supervisor | Which line needs investigation? | A recent disputed report and the resulting action |
| Analyst | Which figure is correct? | Two definitions and their calculation inputs |
| Security lead | Which data may leave the source? | Classification and access constraints |
| Sponsor | Is change worth funding? | Baseline, bounded benefit hypothesis, alternatives |

The diagram connects each concern to evidence. No single enthusiastic stakeholder speaks for every acceptance condition.

<!-- section:deeper -->

Use neutral questions. “How much money would our faster system save?” assumes both the solution and its effect. “What work is delayed, and how is the impact recorded?” leaves room for a different explanation. Summarize what you heard and ask the stakeholder to correct it. A discovery brief should preserve disagreement: two teams can use the same word, such as quality, for different denominators.

<!-- section:customer -->

For an operations sponsor: “When you say real time, what decision becomes different if the data arrives sooner? Could we walk through the last time the delay mattered, including what your team did and how you measured the impact?”

<!-- section:try -->

Fictional stakeholders say: “The dashboard is slow,” “we don't trust the defect percentage,” and “the meeting needs answers by 8 a.m.” Write three neutral questions that separate performance, metric correctness, and freshness.

<!-- section:revisit -->

Ask: “Which interaction is slow, and how long does it take from your click to the answer?” “What numerator, denominator, and exclusions should define the defect percentage?” “When is source data available, and what must be visible by 8 a.m.?”

These questions separate three possible problems. A faster query cannot repair inconsistent definitions; a correct daily total may still arrive after the decision. Validate the summary with each stakeholder before selecting a test. If the sponsor cannot identify a decision changed by fresher data, consider whether simpler reporting improvements are enough.
