<!-- section:why -->

You are joining your first customer call. The account executive asks you to “show the platform.” Before choosing a demo, find the decision the customer needs to make. Your technical contribution is most useful when it reduces an important uncertainty.

By the end, distinguish a product capability, a customer hypothesis, and evidence that would support a decision.

<!-- section:understand -->

Pre-sales field engineering connects a customer's problem to a technically credible path forward. A **capability** is something a platform can do under stated conditions. A **hypothesis** is a belief about how that capability might help this customer. **Evidence** is an observation collected in a defined context. Those are three different things.

For fictional Cinderline Components, “we can build pipelines” is a capability discussion. “A shared quality dataset could make the morning meeting more useful” is a hypothesis. A reconciliation run on agreed sample data would be evidence. A successful demonstration can support the hypothesis, but it cannot establish production reliability across three plants.

Good work makes the next decision easier: name the question, expose assumptions, design a proportional test, and explain what the result does and does not show. You do not need to know every feature. When an answer depends on security, unusual integration, or a contractual commitment, involve the right specialist and retain responsibility for the follow-up.

<!-- section:see -->

**Fictional worked example.** Cinderline's operations director says the 8 a.m. quality meeting uses yesterday's numbers. A feature tour would answer “what exists?” but the immediate decision is “is an hourly quality feed worth investigating?”

| Statement | What it is | What comes next |
|---|---|---|
| We want less time reconciling reports | Desired outcome | Observe today's reconciliation work |
| An hourly dataset may help | Hypothesis | Check when decisions are actually made |
| Sample records reconcile across plants | Possible test result | Inspect scope, exceptions, and remaining risk |

<!-- section:deeper -->

A technically feasible solution may still fail to fit ownership, budget, timing, or the customer's tolerance for change. Include those constraints in the design discussion rather than treating them as someone else's cleanup. Separate a documented fact from a recommendation in your notes. A recommendation can be sensible without being vendor policy. Do not invent an internal leveling rubric or assume a public role title defines decision authority.

<!-- section:customer -->

For an operations audience: “First I want to understand the decision your team makes at 8 a.m. Then we can test whether fresher, reconciled quality data would improve that decision. I will show what we can verify, the assumptions, and anything that still needs specialist review.”

<!-- section:try -->

Cinderline asks, “Can this eliminate all of our reporting delays?” Write a two-sentence response that acknowledges the goal, avoids a guarantee, and proposes one next piece of evidence. Name who should validate it.

<!-- section:revisit -->

A useful response: “We can investigate where the reporting delay occurs, but I cannot promise it disappears before we inspect the source and workflow. Let us compare source availability, pipeline completion, and dashboard refresh times with your data lead, then agree which delay to test.”

The reasoning matters: freshness can be limited before data reaches the platform or after it leaves a pipeline. The proposed evidence separates those causes.
