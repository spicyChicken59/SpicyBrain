<!-- section:why -->

A customer agrees to a pilot. Before anyone builds, decide which claim is worth testing and what result would change the decision. Otherwise the pilot can succeed technically and still leave the sponsor unsure.

<!-- section:understand -->

A proof of value tests selected hypotheses under agreed conditions. It should connect a technical result to a useful decision, without pretending to prove every future benefit. A proof of concept often emphasizes feasibility; the terminology varies, so agree the actual scope rather than relying on the label.

Start with a baseline and a hypothesis. For fictional Cinderline, a hypothesis might be: “A reconciled one-plant quality feed can provide a trusted metric before the morning decision with manageable operating effort.” Break it into measurable parts: correctness, freshness, recovery, and effort. Each part needs its own evidence.

A baseline must be comparable. Use the same business definition, representative input population, measurement endpoints, and relevant workload conditions. If current-state measurement is missing, make baseline collection a first task. Do not invent a convenient number to make the candidate appear valuable.

The diagram makes the readout a decision node with multiple legitimate outcomes, not an automatic expansion path.

<!-- section:deeper -->

Some benefits take longer to observe than a short pilot. Reduced scrap or downtime may require a longer causal study and changes to operating behavior. A short technical pilot can establish prerequisites without proving those downstream savings. Document which claims the test can support and which remain hypotheses. This is original testing guidance, not a vendor-required sales methodology.

<!-- section:customer -->

For the sponsor: “This pilot will test whether the agreed quality data is correct, timely enough, and operable for one plant. We will compare it with the current path. It will not by itself prove a future reduction in scrap or a company-wide financial return.”

<!-- section:try -->

Write one primary hypothesis and two excluded claims for a five-day fictional quality pilot. Name the baseline evidence needed before making a before/after comparison.

<!-- section:revisit -->

A suitable primary hypothesis is that the approved one-plant population can be reconciled and served before the morning decision under agreed operations. Exclude company-wide production reliability and realized scrap savings from the five-day claim. Collect current source-to-report timing, the existing metric definition, and observed reconciliation effort using the same population and measurement method.

A scope that excludes claims can still be valuable: it prevents the team from confusing a useful first test with a guarantee about the whole business.
