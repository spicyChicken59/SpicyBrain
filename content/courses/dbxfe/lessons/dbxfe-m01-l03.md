<!-- section:why -->

A technically interesting pilot can keep expanding without producing a decision. Learn to define a gate: what must be known before the team moves forward, pauses, or changes direction.

<!-- section:understand -->

Think of a customer engagement as a sequence of decisions, with loops when evidence changes the plan. Discovery clarifies a problem. Design proposes a response and alternatives. A demo explains the response. A proof of value tests selected claims. A decision readout interprets the evidence. A delivery handoff carries the accepted scope and unresolved risks forward.

These are learning categories, not mandatory employer sales stages. Some engagements need a short design review; others need a formal test. Pick the smallest evidence step that can resolve the next material uncertainty. Do not run a large pilot just because it looks like progress.

A **decision gate** states the question, evidence required, accepting stakeholder, and possible outcomes. “Demo done” is an activity. “Operations understands the metric and agrees what freshness would be useful” is a more meaningful exit condition. A failed test can still be useful if it changes the design or exposes a requirement early.

<!-- section:see -->

**Fictional gate record.**

| Field | Cinderline example |
|---|---|
| Decision | Invest in a bounded one-plant quality pilot? |
| Evidence needed | Source access confirmed; metric definition agreed; sample reconciliation designed |
| Acceptor | Operations sponsor, with quality, data and security leads |
| Continue | Owners accept the scope and test criteria |
| Pause | Source access or operating owner remains unresolved |
| Change direction | Daily batch already meets the actual business need |

Notice that the record includes a credible “do less” outcome. It prevents the platform narrative from deciding the result in advance.

<!-- section:deeper -->

Evidence has a shelf life and scope. A network review may cover one region and account; it does not transfer automatically to a later architecture. Version the decision record, link the observed result, and retain the reason for a change. At handoff, distinguish accepted behavior from proposed work, and unresolved issues from defects that were fixed. A green demo does not close a production-operability risk.

<!-- section:customer -->

For a mixed business and technical audience: “The next decision is whether this one-plant pilot is worth doing. We will agree the quality metric, confirm a permitted source path, and define the test. If any of those change, we will update the scope rather than quietly expanding it.”

<!-- section:try -->

The fictional pilot reconciles sample data, but no team owns failed daily runs. Should you mark it ready for delivery? Write a decision record with the accepted result, open risk, next owner, and evidence needed to close the risk.

<!-- section:revisit -->

Record sample reconciliation as accepted within its stated scope, while keeping operational ownership open. A possible next action is for the customer data lead and delivery lead to nominate an operator, define alert response and replay procedures, and walk through one failure scenario. Delivery readiness remains conditional on that review.

Do not erase the positive result; do not allow it to hide a different risk. In a follow-up, separate “observed,” “agreed,” and “still required.” That habit keeps the engagement legible when another teammate joins.
